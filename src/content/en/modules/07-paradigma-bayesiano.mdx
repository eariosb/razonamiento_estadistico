## The coin that needs a prior

Before reading, commit to an answer. A coin lands heads 14 times out
of 20 tosses.

The first two answers share a defect: they expect the data to speak
for themselves. The third is the whole Bayesian paradigm. If the
coin came out of your pocket, 14 heads are cheap noise; if a
magician pulled it from a case while betting against you, 14 heads
are close to a confession. Same data, different verdicts, and the
difference lives outside the sample. This module shows the
machinery that makes that difference explicit, honest, and
computable.

## The reasoning people should do

Module M2 introduced Bayes as the correction of an error: the
forgotten base rate. Here the paradigm shows up whole. The
<Term k="prior">prior</Term> is the distribution of beliefs before
the data; the <Term k="verosimilitud">likelihood</Term> is what the
data say about each possible value; the <Term k="posterior">
posterior</Term> is the prior reshaped by the evidence.

The mark that sets the framework apart: belief is an entire
distribution, not a loose number, and it shifts, narrows, or splits
with every observation. Bayes describes the movement of uncertainty,
not just its verdict.

## Prior, data, posterior: the mechanics

With a Beta(α, β) prior over a probability and binary observations,
updating is pure counting: every success adds to α, every failure
adds to β. A uniform Beta(1, 1) prior and 20 tosses with 14 heads
end at Beta(15, 7): the mode sits at 14/20, the mean at 15/22, and
the spread has closed in. The evidence dragged the belief along,
which is what should happen to any reasonable mind.

The code draws that movement: the flat Beta(1,1) prior in dashed
sand, the Beta(15,7) posterior in ochre, and an ink line at the
posterior mode, 14/20. Below it prints the posterior mean and its
90% credibility interval: the updated belief with its honest
uncertainty.

Now watch it move. Pick a prior, feed it tosses one at a time or ten
at a time, and watch the whole posterior slide and sharpen. The coin
has a real hidden bias: the skeptical, the uniform and the
enthusiastic priors start in different places and the data gather
them all in the same spot.

## Lindley's paradox

Lindley (1957) showed that the same data can lead the two paradigms
to opposite conclusions. The paradox needs a specific condition:
the null hypothesis is point-valued (the coin is exactly fair,
p = 0.5) and the prior assigns positive probability mass to that
exact point. With large n and a very spread-out prior over the
alternatives, a result the frequentist test rejects at 5% can carry
a posterior overwhelmingly in favor of the null. The p-value, the
probability of a result at least as extreme if the null were true,
evaluates the data against a single hypothesis; Bayes compares what
the data say about two. It is no logical contradiction: they are
different questions that happen to use the same experiment.

The module's moral goes beyond picking a side: the choice between
paradigms is epistemological: what counts as evidence, what role
prior belief plays, and who carries the burden of proof.

Watch it happen. Pin the evidence at the edge of rejection (z = 1.96)
and push n toward a million: the p-value doesn't flinch while the
posterior hands everything to the null. Move z or the prior's spread
and find where the two paradigms agree again.

## Kahneman versus Gigerenzer

Time to judge the judge. The whole course rested on one thesis:
intuition substitutes questions and fails systematically. This
section puts that thesis to its hardest test, which is also the
debate that organized the field for thirty years. Is the mind
broken, or is the exam unfair?

Two readings of the same experimental building. Kahneman and
Tversky: heuristics produce systematic, measurable errors,
cognitive illusions as real as optical ones. <Term k="Gigerenzer">Gigerenzer</Term>
answers back: heuristics are ecological adaptations that work in
the environments they evolved for; the lab evaluates them with
narrow norms and unnatural formats. And he has data: natural
frequencies "cure" the base-rate fallacy without instruction.

The exchange escalated to a head-on clash in Psychological Review
(1996). Gigerenzer accused the heuristics-and-biases program of
decrying errors without a theory of when each heuristic works:
labels that explain little because they cover everything. Kahneman
and Tversky answered that the critique changed the norm mid-game:
showing that a natural format improves performance does not show
the probabilistic norm was misapplied. Both things are true, and
that is the part the debate usually loses: the error is real and it
is also fixable by changing the presentation, not the mind.

A later adversarial collaboration measured the gap with a shared
method. Mellers, Hertwig and Kahneman (2001) had both camps predict
the outcome of their own conjunction-effect experiments, and both
schools failed to anticipate the public's answers: the theoretical
dispute coexisted with poor empirical calibration on both sides.
Nobody came out intact, which was the point.

The third front is technical. The
<Term k="conjuntos anidados">nested-sets</Term> thesis (Barbey and
Sloman, 2007) proposes that the mind reasons with sets, not with
single-event probabilities: "ten out of a hundred" is processable,
"10% of a person" is not. If probabilistic reasoning operates on
sets, the failures are not defects but format: present the problem
in its natural support and reasoning works. It is the strong version
of the ecological argument.

And there is the positive contribution of Gigerenzer's school:
fast-and-frugal heuristics are not just what fails, they are a
catalog of tools that sometimes win. Recognition, take-the-best
and stopping, "one out of N": rules that spend little information
and that, in environments with the right structure, beat heavier
models. The program does not deny the illusions; it holds that the
same mind that produces them also runs effective inferences, and
that asking "is it rational?" without naming an environment has no
answer.

The course takes both positions as training. The student who can
say when formal rationality and ecological rationality coincide and
when they diverge understood the module better than the one who
memorized a side's verdict.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
The prior nobody writes down is the one that decides: the
probability you give "this well will hit water" before drilling,
"this claim is legitimate" before auditing, "this client will pay"
before approving. When evidence arrives weak, a strong prior rules
the verdict without anyone having justified it, and when it arrives
strong, a stubborn prior wastes it. Making the prior explicit, even
as an uncomfortable range, is half of professional Bayesian hygiene.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The posterior learned to move, and Lindley's paradox showed that
two honest statisticians can disagree without either of them
computing wrong. M8 closes the arc: when the estimator is no longer
a person but an algorithm trained on historical data, the biases
don't vanish. They get amplified or inherited.

**In one sentence:** the right belief is not a verdict but a number that moves: prior plus evidence gives posterior.
