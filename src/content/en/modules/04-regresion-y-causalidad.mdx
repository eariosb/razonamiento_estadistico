## The schools that improved on their own

Before reading, commit to an answer. The ten schools with the worst
average on the national exam enter an improvement program. A year
later their average is up twelve points.

<CommitReveal

    "No: the program did nothing",
    "You cannot tell from this information alone"
  ]}
  correctIndex={2}
>

The honest answer is the third one, and it contains the trap this
module takes apart: selecting by the extreme guarantees that the
next measurement looks better, with or without an intervention.
Twelve points up cannot tell an effective program apart from a
decorative one, because the worst decile of any distribution
improves afterwards by pure chance. Generations of managers,
coaches, and consultants have been paid on this confusion.

</CommitReveal>

## The pilots who disproved the psychology

Kahneman tells how he discovered <Term k="regresión a la media">
regression to the mean</Term> while teaching Israeli Air Force
flight instructors. The instructors were convinced that punishment
worked and praise didn't: after yelling at a pilot for a bad
maneuver, the next one came out better; after praising a good one,
the next came out worse.

The explanation was statistical. Flight performance has a skill
component and a luck component. An exceptionally bad maneuver is,
by definition, an extreme: odds are the next one will be better,
punishment or not. An exceptionally good one, the reverse. Praise
ruined nothing and yelling cured nothing. The mean reclaimed its
territory and the instructors credited the feat to their own voices.

The managerial version of the same phenomenon: a stable quality
process and a management that intervenes every time a line drifts.
You do the rewarding and punishing: the intervened units "improve"
every time, and the intervention collects credit that belongs to
chance.

## Correlations that count for nothing

The pattern you just saw follows a general rule: the mind doesn't
read association, it reads stories. The instructors credited the
yelling for the improvement; here we credit the association with a
cause. When the mind finds two curves moving together, it doesn't
ask what mechanism joins them: it manufactures one. Tyler Vigen
collects <Term k="correlación espuria">spurious
correlations</Term>: per-capita cheese consumption correlates with
civil engineering doctorates awarded; swimming-pool drownings
correlate with the number of Nicolas Cage films released that year.
Correlation coefficients of 0.9 and up, between variables that have
nothing to do with each other.

With enough series and enough time, chance produces perfect
associations. The fallacy is called *cum hoc ergo propter hoc*:
with this, therefore because of this. <Term k="representatividad">
Representativeness</Term> helps the disaster along because System 1
doesn't accept association without a story, and it always finds one.

You don't have to believe it: manufacture it. The explorer below
generates pairs of independent random walks and names them after
Vigen's real archive. It counts how many come out with a "strong"
correlation while having absolutely nothing to do with each other.

## Simpson's paradox

In 1973, the University of Berkeley admitted 44% of male applicants
and 35% of female ones. It looked like discrimination. Disaggregated
by department, the bias vanished or flipped: women applied in larger
share to more selective departments, and within each department the
rates were similar or favored women.

<Term k="paradoja de Simpson">Simpson's paradox</Term> is the reversal
of an association when you disaggregate by a variable. At Berkeley
that variable is a mediator: gender doesn't cause the department,
but it steers the choice, and the department decides admission.
Pearl uses precisely this case to teach that whether to stratify
depends on the causal graph: with a mediator, the stratified view
answers the right question; with a collider, it poisons it. The
aggregate number and the per-stratum number tell opposite stories,
and both are correct: what changes is the question each one answers.

Handle it with your hands. The stacked bars up top show the
confounder at once: where each group applies. Switch the views and
the gap flips; slide "women apply like men" to 100% and the
aggregate gap dissolves without touching a single rate.

## The bridge to formal causality

Three traps, one mechanism. Regression to the mean, Vigen's
correlations, and Simpson's paradox are the same M1 substitution in
another costume: the mind swaps "what process generated this?" for
"what story explains it?". The formal correction exists precisely
because the hard question can be written down.

Pearl (2009) provides the mathematical framework for all of this:
directed causal diagrams separate confounders, mediators, and
colliders, and the back-door criterion says when an association
admits a causal reading. The moral can already be stated without
waiting for the full formalization: seeing association is seeing
shadow, not mechanism. Causality demands a model of the generating
process, not a prettier coefficient.

The code recreates the instructors' trap in two rounds of data:
each unit has a stable level (its skill) plus the luck of the round.
It selects the bottom decile as if it were the group "needing
intervention" and measures those same units on the next round. With
nobody touching anything, the bottom decile "improves" toward the
mean. The imaginary intervention collects credit that belongs to
chance.

<RCell
  id="04/regresion-media"
  title="Regression to the mean: the intervention that doesn't exist"
  code={`# Each unit has a stable level (skill) plus the luck of the round.
# Selecting the bottom decile and "intervening" improves without cause.
set.seed(11)
skill <- rnorm(200, mean = 100, sd = 10)
med1 <- skill + rnorm(200, 0, 15)
med2 <- skill + rnorm(200, 0, 15)

# Select the worst 10% of round 1 and "intervene" (we do nothing).
worst <- order(med1)[1:20]

cat("Bottom-decile mean, round 1:", round(mean(med1[worst]), 2), "\n")
cat("Same units, round 2:        ", round(mean(med2[worst]), 2), "\n")
cat("Mean of all units, round 2: ", round(mean(med2), 2), "\n")
cat("Correlation between rounds: ", round(cor(med1, med2), 2), "\n")`}
/>

The intervened units improve by several points. The intervention was
nothing. That free effect explains a good share of the success that
retraining consultancies, recovery programs, and the flight
instructor's punishments report: intervening on the worst guarantees
apparent improvement, whether the intervention deserves it or not.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
A well with the worst output in the field enters a stimulation
program and pumps better by next quarter. Did the stimulation work?
With ICU patients: yesterday's sickest are better today, therapy or
regression. With claims: the portfolio with the worst loss ratio
this year is the one that gets priced highest next year. Selecting
by the extreme and measuring after is the mechanism that makes
almost any intervention look effective; the right question demands a
comparison group, not just a before and an after.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The do-nothing-intervention simulator at the start of this module is
the same trap in a business suit: the mean reclaims its extremes
while management collects the credit. The other two faces of the
same coin stayed here: the association chance builds for free and
the one that flips when the mediator shows up. M5 raises the stakes:
from pairs of variables to whole processes, where the illusion of
trend makes careers in finance.
