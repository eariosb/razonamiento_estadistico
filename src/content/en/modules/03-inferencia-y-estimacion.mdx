## The number they put in front of you

Before reading, two gestures. Write down the last two digits of your
ID number and multiply them by a hundred: that is your involuntary
starting point. Now estimate in your head how many kilometers the
Nile river is, and keep the number.

>

Your digits know nothing about geography. And yet, in the lab
version of this experiment, estimates shift with the number the
subject saw just before: whoever wrote «07» and whoever wrote «92»
report Niles of different lengths. Tversky and Kahneman proved it
with a rigged wheel that always landed on 10 or 65: those who saw
65 estimated the share of African countries in the UN far higher
than those who saw 10. The Nile is really about 6,650 km, so a
6,000 anchor on top would be useful information, not pure noise.
Your ID, on the other hand, is a perfectly useless anchor, and it
still pulled your estimate.

</CommitReveal>

<Term k="anclaje">Anchoring and adjustment</Term> completes the trio
of heuristics from the 1974 paper: representativeness already showed
up in M1 and availability arrives in M5. Estimates start from the
first number available and adjust toward the right answer, but the
adjustment falls short. In practice it means whoever sets the first
number in a conversation sets the center of gravity for the
negotiation, the forecast, and the diagnosis.

It's worth fixing the module's map before going on. You're about to
measure three gaps in the same instrument, the mind estimating: the
**center** that anchors, the **width** that promises more than it
covers, and the **scatter** nobody audits. The formal statistics at
the end of the module exist because those three gaps were measured,
one by one.

## Intervals that don't cover

If the anchor corrupts the starting point, this section measures
what it does to the width.

Alpert and Raiffa asked Harvard MBA students for 98% confidence
intervals on quantities they didn't know. The intervals promised to
miss 2% of the time; they missed close to 40%. Subjective intervals
come out systematically too narrow. That is
<Term k="sobreconfianza">overconfidence</Term>, and it comes from the
geometry of anchoring applied to uncertainty, not from arrogance.
The central estimate anchors and the endpoints adjust too little.

<Term k="calibración">Calibration</Term> is the opposite property:
stated confidence matching the real frequency of being right. A
well-calibrated meteorologist is right on 80% of the days she
forecasts "80% chance of rain". A badly calibrated executive is
right on 50% of what he states at 90%.

And you? The game below asks you for ten 90% intervals on quantities
you probably don't recall exactly. Then it shows you how many
covered the truth. If your calibration were perfect, you'd miss once
in ten. See how many you miss.

## The outside view

The Alpert and Raiffa intervals failed in the abstract, on quantities
nobody measures daily. The same narrowness has a calendar version
that has probably cost you
money or embarrassment: the <Term k="falacia de planificación">planning
fallacy</Term>. Everyone estimates how long their project will take
from the inside, adding up the tasks they can see, and almost nobody
checks the record of similar projects. Kahneman and Tversky
documented it with textbook authors who estimated a year and a half
and took seven; the monumental version is the Sydney Opera House,
budgeted at six years and finished in fourteen at ten times the
cost.

The antidote has a proper name and is among the most portable in the
course: the <Term k="vista desde afuera">outside view</Term>, or
reference-class forecasting. Before estimating your project, ask
how long projects of its kind took, not how long yours would take
if everything went according to plan. The inside question measures
your optimism; the reference class measures the world.

## Noise: the villain nobody measures

One gap left to measure, the most hidden. Anchoring and
overconfidence show up when you compare yourself against the right
answer; noise is the error invisible even in comparison, because
every wrong answer arrives by a different road.

Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein spent a whole book separating
<Term k="ruido">noise</Term> from bias. Bias pushes decisions in one
systematic direction. Noise scatters them without pattern: two
doctors giving different diagnoses to the same patient, two judges
handing different sentences to the same case, the same analyst
giving different answers on a Monday and a Friday.

Noise is measured with audits, not intuition. And it costs the same
as bias: every judgment different from the right one is an error,
whether it came from a systematic push or a causeless scatter.

Noise also charges a dangerous premium: reacting to it as if it were
signal. Rewarding what rises and punishing what falls in a stable
process produces the illusion of effective management, because the
units you intervened on "improve" every time. That trap has a proper
name, regression to the mean, and a simulator waiting for you at the
start of M4.

## Formal estimation as the correction

The statistical <Term k="IC">confidence interval</Term> exists
precisely because the subjective one fails: it takes the measured
variability of the data and turns uncertainty into a computed range,
not an imagined one. Inference is the institutional answer to
overconfidence.

The code puts that promise before the only test that matters here:
repetition. It generates five thousand samples of size 30 from a
standard normal, computes the 95% CI for each, and records how many
capture the true mean. The cell runs two versions: the naive one
with z = 1.96 and the corrected one with Student's t. The 1.96 falls
short: with the estimated deviation and n = 30, real coverage sits
around 0.94. The t widens the interval just enough to deliver the
promised 0.95. That is what "95% confidence" means: not that this
particular interval holds the parameter, but that the method is
right 95% of the time.

<RCell
  id="03/ic-cobertura"
  title="Real coverage of a 95% interval"
  code={`# Coverage of the 95% CI for a mean with n = 30: z vs. Student's t.
# With estimated sd, z = 1.96 falls short; qt(0.975, n-1) corrects.
set.seed(7)
n <- 30
covers <- function(mult) mean(replicate(5000, {
  x <- rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
  ci <- mean(x) + c(-1, 1) * mult * sd(x) / sqrt(n)
  ci[1] <= 0 & ci[2] >= 0
}))
cat("with z = 1.96:      ", round(covers(1.96), 3), "\n")
cat("with Student's t:   ", round(covers(qt(0.975, n - 1)), 3), "\n")`}
/>

When the model and the multiplier are right, the 95% interval covers
95% of the time; with the naive z it covers less than it promises.
The calibration game above asked the same question about your own
intervals: 90% ranges on uncertain quantities, then the count of how
many covered the real answer.

## From the interval to the p-value

The <Term k="EE">standard error</Term> is the brick under all of
this: the typical deviation of the estimator, not of the data. With
large samples, the sample mean behaves almost like a normal whose
spread is σ/√n, and that's where the interval above comes from.

The <Term k="p">p-value</Term> is built on that same computation and
is, by far, the most misread figure in working statistics. It says
one thing only: if the null hypothesis were true, how likely would
a result at least as extreme as yours be. It does not say what the
probability of the null being true is, the question everyone wants
answered and the one that requires a prior, the road M7 takes. It
doesn't measure effect size or practical importance either: with
enough n, an insignificant difference produces a brilliant p-value.

<Term k="potencia">Power</Term> is the other face of the design: the
probability of detecting the effect if it exists. An underpowered
study that finds nothing has not proven absence; it has proven it
was too small. When no theoretical distribution convinces, the
<Term k="bootstrap">bootstrap</Term> remains: resampling your own
data to build the spread empirically. M6 develops it properly.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
Anchoring is the first number in a reserves assessment, the opening
bid of a mining tender, the first flow reading a hydrologist sees.
And a p-value of 0.04 over twenty drilled wells does not mean "4%
chance the deposit doesn't exist": it means that, if it didn't,
data like this would appear 4 out of 100 times. The commercial
question is a different one, and only the base rate of your region
plus the new evidence answers it, as in M2.
</Callout>
