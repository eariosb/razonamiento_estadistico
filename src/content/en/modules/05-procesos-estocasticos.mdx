## Which one came from a coin?

Before reading, commit to an answer. One of these two sequences came
from tossing a fair coin thirty times. The other was written by a
person imitating randomness:

```
A: H T H H T H T T H T H T H H T H T H T H H T H T T T H T H T
B: H H T T H H T T H H T T H H T T H H T T H T H T H T H T H T
```

Most people pick B, because it alternates evenly and shows no long
streaks, and they are wrong. In a fair coin, streaks are the norm:
in thirty tosses, seeing four heads in a row at some point is
typical, not an anomaly. The fabricated sequence avoids streaks
because the eye reads them as pattern. Whoever imitates randomness
systematically over-alternates, and that is the fingerprint that
gives them away: B was written by a hand, A came out of the coin.

It's the same rule as M1: people judge a random sequence by how
"messy" it looks, not by the distribution that generated it. This
module carries that intuition into processes that move through time.

</CommitReveal>

## The walk that looks like it's going somewhere

Generate a pure <Term k="caminata aleatoria">random walk</Term>: each
step is an independent draw, no trend and no memory in the
increments; the level you draw, though, accumulates the whole past.
Plot it. Now look at it with a market analyst's eyes: you'll find
clear trends, channels, supports, ceiling breakouts. All of that is
in the chart. None of it is in the process.

The random walk produces apparent structure for free because it
accumulates: a few steps in the same direction are enough to draw a
"trend" that lasts months on the chart. The accumulated variance
grows with time, so long excursions from the origin are normal, not
anomalous.

The code draws exactly that: six random walks of 365 steps, each the
running sum of zero-drift normal steps. The ochre line is simply the
first of the six; the dashed slate marks the origin. Look for the
trend, the support, the breakout: they're all there, and none of
them exists.

offers each one a story. The process has no story: it has
<Term k="ruido blanco">white noise</Term> piling up.

promised: not a direction but a cone of futures, dense at the center
and growing wider as the square root of time.

## Trend, seasonality, and noise

The walk manufactures trends for free. The next question comes
naturally: if chance draws structure, how do you tell real structure
apart? A real series mixes three ingredients the eye separates poorly:
trend (the underlying direction), seasonality (the cycles that
repeat) and noise (everything else). The decomposition is a modeling
decision, not a fact of the chart: two honest analysts can split the
same series differently. Time series demand thinking in processes,
and thinking in processes is exactly what System 1 avoids.

The property that orders the territory is
<Term k="estacionariedad">stationarity</Term>: that the statistical
behavior of the process doesn't change over time. The random walk is
not stationary, and that is the technical reason its "trends" come
free.

## Availability: the risk that makes the news

So far the module measured illusions in the data. Now it measures
one in your memory, and with it the 1974 trio is complete:
representativeness made you fail in M1, anchoring in M3, and this
third heuristic was the one still missing.

What causes more deaths per year: plane crashes or household
accidents? The <Term k="disponibilidad">availability heuristic</Term>
(Tversky and Kahneman, 1973) predicts the typical answer and its
error: whatever comes to mind easily is judged frequent. Plane
crashes make the news every year; household accidents kill more and
have no correspondents.

In time series, availability keeps a calendar: analysts overestimate
the probability of what just happened (the last crisis, the team's
last failure) and underestimate what hasn't occurred in the recent
past. The recent record becomes the model, and the model collects
on the next surprise.

Measure yourself. Five pairs of causes of death, one question each:
which kills more? Your answers, like almost everyone's, will register
the headlines instead of the rates.

## The hot hand, recounted

The module's arc closes here. The fake sequences at the opening
over-alternated; the walk manufactured trends. The hot hand is the
case where the pattern illusion was measured seriously, and where
statistics itself fell into one of the course's own traps.

The classic study by Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky (1985) measured
scoring streaks in the NBA and found no hot hand: after a make, the
probability of the next shot was the same as after a miss. For
thirty years it was the flagship example of "intuition sees patterns
in noise".

In 2018, Miller and Sanjurjo showed the classic analysis had a bias
of its own, subtle and geometric: conditioning on "the shot after a
make" inside a finite sequence is not neutral. The attempts that
meet that condition are a biased sample, because a sequence that
ends in a make contributes no next shot. With the selection bias
corrected, the same data show a small but real hot hand, on the
order of a few percentage points.

The lesson doubled: collective intuition did exaggerate the effect,
and the statistics that debunked it made the error this course has
repeated since M2, forgetting which set you're conditioning on. The
honest verdict today is that the effect exists and is much smaller
than the crowd feels. Distrusting the pattern is correct; concluding
"zero effect" required a calculation that was done wrong.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
Streaks are professional temptation: the operator with three
accident-free shifts "is on a roll", the fund that won six straight
quarters "has the right manager", the recruiter who landed two
hires "figured it out". This module's question is always the same:
is the streak a signal that the process changed, or the normal
signature of one that didn't? Long streaks are expected in
independent sequences, and treating them as evidence of momentum
costs decisions.
</Callout>
