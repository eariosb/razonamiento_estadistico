## Ninety-five percent accuracy

Before reading, commit to an answer. A bank trains an anti-fraud
model with 95% accuracy on a million transactions, of which one in a
thousand is fraudulent. The model flags your transaction as fraud.

If you picked something near 95%, the module has already made its
point: global accuracy is the PPV in disguise, and M2 already taught
you what happens when the base rate is low. The count: of a
thousand transactions there is 1 fraud and 999 legitimate ones; the
model catches almost all the fraud but also flags a small
percentage of legitimate activity, and that small percentage of 999
dwarfs the single real fraud. The exact number depends on its
error rates, but the conclusion doesn't: a marker "95% accurate"
over rare events produces more false alarms than catches.

## Biases don't vanish with more data

Machine learning's implicit promise is that volume cures: with
enough data, the right pattern emerges on its own. The whole course
has worked to make that sentence sound suspicious. Volume amplifies
what's there: if the data carry the base rate of an unequal society,
the model learns it more precisely and applies it at greater scale.

The case that put it on the cover was COMPAS (Angwin et al., 2016,
*Machine Bias*): among defendants who did **not** reoffend, the
software flagged 45% of Black defendants as high risk versus 23% of
white ones. The figure is conditional, on the true negatives, and
that's where it hurts. Northpointe's reply was just as serious: the
model was equally calibrated in both groups; a "high risk" meant the
same for each. Both claims are true at once, and that is the core of
the debate. Not a single line of code mentioned race.
<Term k="sesgo algorítmico">Algorithmic bias</Term> doesn't need
explicit racism: a differential base rate between groups and honest
proxy variables are enough.

## Breiman's two cultures

Breiman (2001) named the split before ML inherited it: data modeling
wants to understand the mechanism (the box must explain); algorithmic
modeling wants to predict well (the box just has to work). The
<Term k="dos culturas">two cultures</Term> produce different criteria
for success and, above all, different criteria for failure. A model
that predicts without explaining may be discriminating; one that
explains without predicting may be telling a good story.

AUC summarizes discrimination, not justice nor
<Term k="calibración">calibration</Term>: a model can rank well on
average and still assign systematically inflated risks to a group,
exactly what Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan formalize by showing that
definitions of fairness are mutually incompatible except in special
cases.

## Predicting well: overfitting and validation

Overfitting is the law of small numbers wearing silicon: a model
with enough degrees of freedom memorizes the sample's noise and
reports it as signal. The symptom is always the same: brilliant
performance on data it already saw and collapse on data it didn't.
The defense is equally uniform and worth more than any algorithm:
measure performance on data the model never touched.
<Term k="validación">Cross-validation</Term> institutionalizes that
distrust, rotating which part of the sample trains and which part
judges, so that no success counts if it was seen during learning.

The second professional error is believing the model decides. The
model assigns a number; you decide by setting the threshold. And the
right threshold is not a statistical number, it's a ratio of costs:
how much a false positive hurts against how much a false negative
hurts. In the opening fraud case, rejecting legitimate transactions
costs clients and letting fraud through costs money, so the
threshold moves until expected cost, not accuracy, is minimized.
Two hospitals with the same model and different costs should use
different thresholds, and that's no defect of the model; it's the
decision placed where it always belonged: with whoever pays the
consequences.

## Deep learning and the promise that remains

Deep networks changed the landscape because they learn the very
representation of the data, not just the boundary over given
representations. For this course one precise consequence matters:
the more expressive the model, the more of the course's confusion
mechanisms it inherits. It overfits at scale, absorbs historical
base rates with higher fidelity, and its opacity turns into
research what in a regression was reading a coefficient. Breiman's
two-cultures lesson hardens: predicting very well while explaining
badly is, for most of the decisions this course discusses, exactly
the wrong combination.

## Meehl, seventy years earlier

The debate between human judgment and algorithm didn't start with
machine learning. Meehl (1954) compared clinical prediction, the
expert weighing the whole case, with actuarial prediction, a
statistical rule over the same variables, across dozens of domains.
The result was uncomfortable and has replicated for seventy years:
the empirical rule wins or ties almost always, and rarely loses.
Dawes extended it by showing that even "improper" models, rounded
weights, beat expert judgment.

The honest reading has two faces and both matter. Against human
enthusiasm: if you're going to override the algorithm, the burden
of proof is yours, not its. Against algorithmic enthusiasm: Meehl's
rules were simple, auditable, and stable; the modern model buys its
edge in accuracy at the price of exactly the fragilities this module
lists. The course's closing criterion decides where to trust: an
environment with regularities and feedback calls for the systematic
method; an irregular environment exempts neither intuition from
review.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
The claims model with 96% accuracy that never flags anything in a
portfolio with 1% claims is playing the opening game: beating the
majority without looking at anyone. The credit score that passes
internal validation but collapses on the segment the sample never
covered repeats overfitting in institutional clothing. And whoever
sets the credit-approval threshold is making a cost decision in
statistical disguise: every point of the cutoff trades how many good
clients are lost against how many bad ones get in.
</Callout>

## Noise, now algorithmic

The <Term k="ruido">noise</Term> of *Noise* translates directly:
where ten human judges diverged, ten trainings of the "same" model
diverge. The precision Meehl and Dawes ask for is due here: an
already-trained mechanical rule has no noise at decision time; the
same case gets the same number every time. The variability appears
earlier, in the modeling: the seed, the cleaning pipeline, the
training sample. It's real noise, but of another category:
construction noise, not judgment noise. The algorithmic promise
removes the first; the second only appears when someone decides to
train the model ten times with the same yardstick used to audit the
human.

Watch it in two lanes. The same case goes through ten human judges
and through ten versions of the "same" model: different seed,
different pipeline, same purpose. Human noise is visible at a
glance; algorithmic noise only shows up when someone decides to
train it ten times.

<NoisyJudgeSim />

The code reduces the mechanism of algorithmic bias to its skeleton:
two groups of ten thousand people with different outcome
prevalences (50% and 10%), a score that does look at the individual
(mean 1.5 for reoffenders, 0 for non-reoffenders) and an identical
threshold for both. Then it computes three conditional rates per
group: who gets flagged while negative, who gets flagged while
positive, and what fraction of the flagged is truly positive. The
model never read the group's name. Even so, one of the rates comes
out different.

<RCell
  id="08/tasa-base-diferencial"
  title="Same classifier, two groups, two error rates"
  code={`# The same classifier over two groups with different prevalences.
# The score does look at the individual: mean 1.5 if reoffender, 0 if not.
# Within each group the conditional distributions are identical,
# so FPR and TPR come out equal; what breaks is the PPV.
set.seed(21)
n <- 10000
group <- rep(c("A", "B"), each = n / 2)
prevalence <- ifelse(group == "A", 0.5, 0.1)
y <- rbinom(n, 1, prevalence)
score <- rnorm(n, mean = 1.5 * y, sd = 1)  # informed by the individual
pred_pos <- score > 1                      # identical threshold in both

# Conditional rates by group: counting hits is not enough; look at
# who pays the error.
fpr <- tapply(pred_pos[y == 0], group[y == 0], mean)   # false positives among negatives
tpr <- tapply(pred_pos[y == 1], group[y == 1], mean)   # true positives among positives
ppv <- tapply(y[pred_pos], group[pred_pos], mean)      # precision among the flagged

cat("P(flagged | negative) by group:", round(fpr, 3), "\n")
cat("P(flagged | positive) by group:", round(tpr, 3), "\n")
cat("P(positive | flagged) by group:", round(ppv, 3), "\n")`}
/>

The result is the reverse of what intuition expects: the model does
use the base rate and respects the individual (FPR and TPR come out
equal across groups), and still "flagged" doesn't mean the same for
both. In the low-prevalence group, a flagged case is truly positive
far less often. It's the M2 lesson turned into a system: with
different prevalences and the same threshold, there's no way to
equalize all the metrics at once. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and
Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017) proved it as a theorem: you
can equalize one, never all.

Here is the full mechanism. Two groups, the same classifier, the
same threshold; only the prevalence changes. Move group B's base
rate and watch which metric breaks. Then try to repair it with the
threshold: you'll find you can equalize one metric, never all at
once.

## Closing the course: the skill of knowing when to doubt

Eight modules ago, the first Monty Hall door looked like a tie to
you. This course's promise was never to make you infallible: the
experiments say not even experts are. The promise was something
else, more useful: teaching you to recognize the terrain where your
intuition loses. Forgotten base rates, small samples taken for large
ones, conjunctions that seduce, means that chance returns, trends
that noise draws, algorithms that inherit the history they shouldn't.

Statistics is a cognitive technology. It exists because the mind
doesn't ship with it, and the AI that came later runs the same race
with worse-documented habits: it amplifies the bad question and the
bad data more efficiently than any human. The professional this
course set out to train computes well, certainly, but above all
knows when to distrust their own certainty, human or computed.

And here the last nuance is due, because a course that only teaches
doubt produces another bias, universal distrust. Kahneman and Klein
(2009), the two captains of opposing camps, sat down to define when
intuition is genuinely expert and reached a two-condition criterion:
the environment has valid regularities to learn, and the person had
prolonged practice with fast, unambiguous feedback. The firefighter
reading the fire and the nurse reading the vein meet both; the
analyst forecasting markets, the judge estimating recidivism, and
whoever evaluates a stranger in an interview meet neither. That is
the closing criterion: don't distrust intuition, verify its
conditions of production.

Seen from a distance, the course's eight traps are a single one,
the one Kahneman and Frederick named back in M1: the mind answers
the easy question instead of the hard one. Linda was resemblance
for probability, the M2 test diagnosis for disease, the M3 interval
certainty for sample size, the M4 improvement cause for luck, the
M5 streak pattern for real frequency, the M6 median the available
answer for the asked one, the M7 p-value the inverted hypothesis,
and this module's algorithm accuracy for the right decision. The
final hygiene is a single question, repeatable out loud on every
terrain: what question am I actually answering?
