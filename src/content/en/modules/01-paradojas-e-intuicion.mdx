This module opens with four problems designed to make you fail: three
doors, a woman named Linda, a chord inside a circle, and a coin that
refuses to behave. The point is to feel the stumble inside your own
head before anyone explains why it happened. Psychologists call that
cognitive strain: for our purposes, it is the starting point.

## Three doors, a car, and two goats

The game show is simple. Three closed doors. Behind one, a car;
behind the other two, goats. You pick a door. The host, who knows
where the car is, opens another door and shows you a goat. Then he
asks the question that has broken friendships: do you stick with
your door or switch to the one still closed?

Almost everyone answers that it doesn't matter. Two closed doors,
one car, fifty-fifty. Play a few rounds and check your own track
record before reading on.

<MontyHallGame />

Played already? Look at your numbers. Chances are you don't see a
clear difference between staying and switching: with a handful of
rounds, luck covers the advantage, and expecting your history to
reveal it is the law of small numbers working again. The massive
simulation does pull them apart: after ten thousand rounds, staying
wins about a third of the time and switching wins two-thirds.

## The trap explained

Your first pick is right with probability $1/3$. Nobody disputes
that. The question is what happens to that probability when the host
opens a door with a goat.

Intuition reasons like this: two doors remain, the car is behind one
of them, fifty-fifty. The mistake is treating the host as if he
opened doors at random. He doesn't. He knows where the car is and
always opens a goat. His move doesn't split the probability between
the two doors: it concentrates it.

Your door keeps its original $1/3$, because the rules said the host
would never open it. The $2/3$ that belonged to the other two doors
together didn't vanish when one opened: it migrated, whole, to the
single closed door left. Switching doesn't give you better luck. It
hands you the full inheritance of the door that was sacrificed.

<Callout type="note" title="The detail everyone misses">
If the host opened a door at random and it happened to be a goat by
luck, this would be a different problem, and there switching or
staying really would be a coin flip. The trap works because he knows
and because you know he knows. In probability, the rules of the game
are part of the problem, even when the problem doesn't shout them.
</Callout>

</CommitReveal>

Verify it with R. The code simulates the full mechanics of the show:
it draws the prize door and your first pick, lets the host open a
goat that isn't yours (he knows where the car is), and records what
each strategy would have won on the same round. Ten thousand rounds
later, the win rates of "stay" and "switch" converge to their
theoretical values:

<RCell
  id="01/monty-hall"
  title="Monty Hall simulated: stay vs. switch"
  code={`# Monty Hall: 10000 rounds modeling the full mechanism.
set.seed(2024)

# Careful: sample(x) on a length-1 x samples from 1:x, not from x.
pick <- function(x) if (length(x) == 1) x else sample(x, 1)

play <- function() {
  prize  <- sample(1:3, 1)
  first  <- sample(1:3, 1)
  # The host opens a goat that is not your door (he knows the car).
  opens  <- pick(setdiff(1:3, c(first, prize)))
  second <- setdiff(1:3, c(first, opens))  # the only closed door left
  c(stay = first == prize, switch = second == prize)
}

results <- replicate(10000, play())
rowMeans(results)`}
/>

## Linda, the bank teller

Second trap, different format. Read the description carefully before
answering.

Tversky and Kahneman gave this problem to graduate students in 1983
and the results were uncomfortable. Now it's your turn.

Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and very bright. She studied
philosophy, cared about discrimination and social justice, and took
part in anti-nuclear demonstrations. Which is more likely? First:
Linda is a bank teller. Second: Linda is a bank teller and is active
in the feminist movement.

<LindaVenn />

The fallacy has a proper name: <Term k="falacia de conjunción">the
conjunction fallacy</Term>. Joining two conditions in one statement
makes it more vivid, more coherent with the stereotype, more
narratively satisfying. It also makes it less likely, always, by
pure set logic. The intersection cannot exceed the set.

The engine of the error is the <Term k="representatividad">representativeness
heuristic</Term>: judging probability by resemblance. Linda looks far
more like the "feminist" stereotype than the "bank teller" one, so
the conjunction, which holds the detail that fits best, feels truer.
Resemblance measures narrative fit. Probability measures something
else.

<Quiz
  question="Why can't 'Linda is a bank teller and a feminist' be more likely than 'Linda is a bank teller'?"
  options={[
    "Because being a feminist is statistically rare among bank tellers",
    "Because the set 'teller and feminist' sits inside the set 'teller', and a subset never exceeds its set",
    "Because Linda's description was designed to deceive",
    "Because P(A ∩ B) is always zero when A and B are independent"
  ]}
  correctIndex={1}
  explanation="Set logic allows no exceptions: if a case meets two conditions, it also meets each one separately. Adding a condition can only shrink or hold the probability, never raise it. The stereotyped description is the bait; set logic is the law."

## Two more doors in the same lobby

Before going on, it's worth seeing that the doors are only the
stage: the real problem is what intuition does to probability
itself.

**Bertrand's paradox** (1889). Take a circle, draw a chord at
random, and ask: what's the probability the chord is longer than the
side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? There are at least three
legitimate ways to read "at random": by the angle of the endpoints,
by the chord's distance to the center, by the position of its
midpoint inside the circle. Each gives a different answer, in that
order: $1/3$, $1/2$ and $1/4$. All three are correct. The paradox
lives in the question, not the calculation: "at random" does not
define itself.

**The St. Petersburg paradox** (Bernoulli, 1738). A coin is tossed
until it lands heads. If that happens on the first toss you're paid
2, on the second 4, on the third 8, doubling on and on. The game's
expected value is infinite. How much would you pay to play? Almost
nobody offers more than 20 units, and they're right not to: the
astronomical prize that inflates the average almost never arrives.
Expected value is a summary, not a universal guide.

Bernoulli's escape from his own paradox coined a concept this course
needed to define: <Term k="utilidad">utility</Term>, what money is
worth to the person receiving it rather than what it weighs on a
scale. A thousand more units changes the life of someone with little
and changes almost nothing for someone with plenty, so utility grows
slower than wealth. With logarithmic utility the game stops being
worth infinity and drops to a few dozen, exactly what people pay.
Two and a half centuries later, Kahneman and Tversky flipped the
question: not how decisions should be made, but how they are made.
<Term k="teoría prospectiva">Prospect theory</Term> (1979), the work
behind the Nobel, collects four findings: choices are made from a
reference point, not from total wealth; losses hurt more than
equivalent gains feel good (loss aversion); the same outcome is
chosen or rejected depending on how it's framed, lives saved or
lives lost; and probabilities don't weigh linearly, the very small
get inflated and the very large get deflated. The anchor of M3 and
the base rate of M2 describe errors of estimation; this is an error
of choice, the last member of the family.

The two paradoxes teach the same lesson from different angles:
probability only exists once the space of possibilities is defined,
and an average can hide everything that matters. Intuition skips
both details with astonishing ease.

## The law of small numbers

Last trap of the module, and the most dangerous because it dresses
as common sense. Tversky and Kahneman called it the <Term k="LNP">law
of small numbers</Term>: the belief that a small sample must resemble
the population. That ten tosses of a fair coin should come out near
five heads. That a small hospital can't have strange streaks.

Try the experiment. A thousand repetitions of tossing a fair coin
n times, with n of your choosing:

<SmallNumbersLab />

With n = 10, seven heads is not news, it's routine. With n = 1000,
the same proportion would be a scandal of more than twelve standard
deviations. Chance does not correct itself quickly; it dilutes
slowly. Intuition confuses those two things and expects the small
sample to behave like the large one.

This law has a wicked cousin, the <Term k="falacia del jugador">gambler's
fallacy</Term>: if the law of small numbers expects the sample to
match the population right now, then after five heads, tails is
"due". The coin has no memory. We lend it ours.

## System 1 and System 2

The four traps in this module look different. They share the same
mechanism. <Term k="Kahneman">Kahneman</Term> describes it as a split
between two systems.

<Term k="S1">System 1</Term> is fast, automatic, and cheap. It reads
Linda's description and delivers a verdict before you finish the
sentence. It sees two closed doors and declares a tie. It sees five
heads and announces tails is "due". It's brilliant at narratives and
terrible at frequencies.

<Term k="S2">System 2</Term> is slow, effortful, and lazy. It can run
the correct computation, but it doesn't switch itself on: you have
to call it. All statistical training amounts, at bottom, to training
System 2 to show up for work at the exact moments System 1 is about
to fail.

Beneath the split sits a more precise mechanism that ties together
every trap in the course. Kahneman and Frederick (2002) called it
<Term k="sustitución de atributos">attribute substitution</Term>:
when the question is hard, the mind answers an easier one and
delivers that answer as if it were the one asked. Linda swaps
"probability" for "resemblance". Monty Hall swaps "what does his
knowing tell me" for "how many doors are left". The law of small
numbers swaps "how variable is a small sample" for "it looks like
the large one". Kahneman himself admitted System 1 and System 2 are
fictional characters, narrative shortcuts; substitution is the real
process their costumes explain.

Hold on to that idea: it's the course's master key. Every module
ahead is the same substitution in another costume. The base rate gets
dropped because "how good is the test" is easier than "how likely is
sick". The anchor pulls because "how close to adjust" is easier than
"what range does the evidence justify". And in M8 you'll see the
algorithm inherit the same operation: it answers the easy question it
was written instead of the hard one the case demanded.

<Callout type="warning" title="Bias is not cured by knowledge">
The original experiments were run on graduate students, physicians,
and professional statisticians. They failed like everyone else.
Kahneman's uncomfortable conclusion: bias is the default response
of a mind that evolved for other tasks, and no training removes it
entirely. That's why this course makes you fail live: a concept
sticks better when it cost you.
A note of academic honesty: the findings in this course are the ones
that survived replication. Not every chapter of *Thinking, Fast and
Slow* had that luck; social priming, for instance, did not survive
the mass replications. The course leaves them out on purpose.
</Callout>

The lab takes the simulation one step further. The first part replays
Monty Hall with four doors and two different host rules (open one
goat or open two) so you see that the switching advantage is no magic
number: it is the probability the host concentrates on the closed
doors. The second part tosses a fair coin (the case where the true
probability is known in advance) and repeats the experiment of
tossing it n times twenty thousand times over, with n = 10, 100 and
1000. In each repetition it records whether the sample reached 70%
heads.

<LabExercise
  id="lab-01-tu-propia-trampa"
  title="Your own trap: simulate, then explain"
  code={`# Lab: reproduce the module's traps with R.
set.seed(42)

# 1. Monty Hall simulated properly: the host OPENS goats knowingly.
#    Staying keeps 1/N; each door the host leaves closed inherits an
#    equal share of the probability your pick failed to capture.
# Careful: sample(x) on a length-1 x samples from 1:x, not from x.
pick <- function(x, n = 1) if (length(x) == 1) rep(x, n) else sample(x, n)

play <- function(n_doors, n_revealed) {
  prize      <- sample.int(n_doors, 1)
  first      <- sample.int(n_doors, 1)
  candidates <- setdiff(seq_len(n_doors), first)
  opened     <- pick(setdiff(candidates, prize), n_revealed)
  second     <- pick(setdiff(candidates, opened))  # switch to a closed door
  c(stay = first == prize, switch = second == prize)
}
for (case in list(c(3, 1), c(4, 1), c(4, 2))) {
  res <- replicate(20000, play(case[1], case[2]))
  cat(sprintf("%d doors, opens %d goat(s) -> stay: %.3f | switch: %.3f\n",
              case[1], case[2], mean(res["stay", ]), mean(res["switch", ])))
}

# 2. The law of small numbers: a fair coin (p = 0.5) is the clean
#    case: the true probability is known, so any deviation from 50%
#    is pure chance. rbinom(20000, n, 0.5) repeats the experiment of
#    tossing the coin n times 20000 times; each element of p is the
#    head proportion of one repetition, and mean(p >= 0.7) counts how
#    often a sample reaches 70% heads.
for (n in c(10, 100, 1000)) {
  p <- rbinom(20000, n, 0.5) / n
  cat(sprintf("n = %4d -> sd(p) = %.3f | P(70%% heads or more) = %.4f\n",
              n, sd(p), mean(p >= 0.7)))
}`}
  tasks={[
    "Verify the simulation by hand: staying wins 1/N and switching wins (N−1)/N shared across the doors left closed. Explain why with 4 doors and one goat opened switching reaches 3/8, but when the host opens two goats it reaches 3/4.",
    "The second part simulates a fair coin because it is the cleanest random process there is: the true head probability is known (0.5), so any deviation from 50% can only be chance. The measured event is 'reaching 70% heads', which means 7 heads if you tossed 10 times and 700 if you tossed 1000: the same proportion at a different scale. Compare what the simulation reports for each n and explain why the same 70% goes from routine to scandal. What would you tell someone who generalizes from ten observations?",
    "Modify the simulation so the host picks which door to open at random (he might reveal the prize): what happens to the switching advantage, and why?",
  ]}
  hint="With four doors, your initial pick hits 1/4; if the host opens a goat, the remaining 3/4 is shared by two closed doors: each inherits 3/8, still better than 1/4. The switching advantage always exists, but its size depends on how much probability the host concentrates on the closed doors. If he opened at random, seeing a goat does change your probability, because he could have opened the car."
