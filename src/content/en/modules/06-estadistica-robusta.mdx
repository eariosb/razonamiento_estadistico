## The premium that picks the estimator

Before reading, commit to an answer. You run an insurance company
and must set this line's premium. Last year's claims have a median
of two million and a mean of eleven, because a single catastrophic
claim dragged the average.

<CommitReveal
  question="Which of the two summaries do you price the premium with?"
  options={[
    "The median: the extreme claim is an accident, not the typical case",
    "The mean: the extreme claim is also paid by the portfolio"
  ]}
  correctIndex={1}
>

The mean. The premium does not ask about the typical claim but about
the portfolio's total cost, and the catastrophic claim comes out of
that account. Whoever prices with the median charges two million per
policy and goes broke the first time the two-hundred-million one
arrives. But the answer dismantles an idea this very module is about
to build first: that the median is the most honest summary. Honest
for what: that is the question that decides everything, and the
answer changes with what you mean to protect.

</CommitReveal>

## The outlier that drags the mean

Take twenty honest delivery times, around 48 hours. Add one bad day:
a lost package that took 600. The mean jumps from 48 to nearly 75.
The <Term k="mediana">median</Term> barely moves.

The mean has <Term k="punto de quiebre">breakdown point</Term> zero:
a single contaminated data point, however extreme, can take it to
any value. The median resists until almost half the data is
corrupted before giving in. That difference is far more than a
technicality: it's the formal answer to a defective intuition. The
direction of the bias depends on how the risk arrives: when extremes
are known by description, people overweight them; when they arrive
through firsthand experience, like a data sample, people
underestimate them. Hertwig and colleagues (2004) documented that
gap between decisions from description and decisions from
experience. Robust statistics corrects the second case: data arrive
undescribed and the extremes pass as rare, which they are not.

Feel it with your hands. The ochre dot below is the lost package:
drag it upward with your finger or the arrow keys and watch the mean
chase it live while the median stays put.

## Real data don't read textbooks

If the median holds where the mean falls, the right question comes
up: why not always use it? Because protection has a price, and the
price is treating the extreme as noise. The normal rules intuition because it rules the examples. Working
data have <Term k="colas pesadas">heavy tails</Term>: delivery times,
financial returns, insured claims, extreme rainfall. In a heavy
tail, outliers are the most important information in the
distribution, far from being measurement errors to clean up. Deleting
them destroys the data point carrying the most decision weight.

The companion measures change with the distribution. The standard
deviation squares deviations and the extremes inflate it; the
<Term k="MAD">median absolute deviation</Term> counts them head-on
and survives.

The same thing happens to hypothesis tests. The comparator below
pits the t-test against the rank test under the same experiment,
four hundred times per level. With clean data the t wins, as it
should. Raise the tail slider and watch which of the two still sees
the difference once normality leaves.

## The question picks the estimator

Here the opening premium gets resolved, and with it the module's
apparent tension. If robust statistics "corrects" by summarizing
with the median, how can it say two paragraphs later that the
extreme point is the most important information? Both statements
are true because they answer different questions.

If the question is "what happens to the typical case?", the median:
an individual customer, the ordinary delivery time, the common
claim. If the question is "what does the phenomenon cost in
total?", the mean: the portfolio's aggregate cost, the annual
budget, the premium. The insurer pricing with the median charges
two million where the expected claim is eleven, and goes broke in
the first bad year. The extreme the median ignores so elegantly is
exactly the one the premiums pay for.

And there's a technical caveat that closes the circle: the
bootstrap in the next section estimates a statistic's uncertainty
by resampling the data, but for the mean it fails precisely where
it would matter most, in tails so heavy that the true variance is
infinite. No finite resample reaches it. Robustness is not an
ideology in favor of the median: it's knowing what each statistic
protects and what price that protection charges.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
Pricing premiums with the median claim, budgeting a mining project
with the typical cost, sizing water reserves with the median
rainfall: in all three cases the robust summary of the typical case
answers the wrong question, because the risk lives in the tail. And
conversely: summarizing a neighborhood's income with the mean
contaminates the typical case with a few extremes. Question first,
statistic second.
</Callout>

## The bootstrap: inference without a distribution

When no theoretical distribution convinces, the
<Term k="bootstrap">bootstrap</Term> remains: resample your own data
with replacement, recompute the statistic thousands of times, and
use the resulting spread as the empirical sampling distribution.
Efron formalized it in 1979 and it is, at bottom, the course's lab
turned into method: when intuition and theory fail, simulate.

The code does both things in one run. First it builds the scene:
twenty honest delivery times plus a lost 600-hour package, and pits
mean and standard deviation against median and MAD. Then it
resamples those twenty-one data points with replacement five
thousand times and computes the bootstrap standard error of each
estimator, the measure of how much each can dance from one sample
to another. The mean dances a lot; the median, barely.

<RCell
  id="06/media-vs-mediana"
  title="One outlier against mean and median"
  code={`# 20 honest times + one lost package.
set.seed(3)
deliveries <- c(rnorm(20, mean = 48, sd = 4), 600)

cat("Mean:   ", round(mean(deliveries), 1), "h\n")
cat("Median: ", round(median(deliveries), 1), "h\n")
cat("SD:     ", round(sd(deliveries), 1), "h\n")
cat("MAD:    ", round(mad(deliveries), 1), "h\n")

# Bootstrap of the mean and the median.
B <- 5000
b <- replicate(B, {
  x <- sample(deliveries, replace = TRUE)
  c(mean(x), median(x))
})
cat("\nBootstrap SE of the mean:  ", round(sd(b[1, ]), 1), "\n")
cat("Bootstrap SE of the median:", round(sd(b[2, ]), 1), "\n")`}
/>

The mean reaches 74 hours because of a single package; the median
stays at 48. And the bootstrap shows the second effect: under
contamination, the mean also varies far more across resamples. The
outlier doesn't just move the number, it makes it unstable.

## What comes next

The fragility is now measured: a single point moves the mean, a
fraction of tail brings down the t-test, and the order of the data
withstands both attacks. M7 changes paradigms entirely: from the
resistant estimator to the updated belief.

**In one sentence:** question first, statistic second; the median describes the typical case and the mean accumulates the cost.
