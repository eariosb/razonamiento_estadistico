## The test almost everyone reads wrong

A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test exists with 99%
<Term k="sensibilidad">sensitivity</Term> and 95%
<Term k="especificidad">specificity</Term>. You take it and it comes
back positive.

In the original study (Casscells et al., 1978), run on staff and

factor of fifty, and seasoned physicians make it. The explorer below
does the count with a thousand people, one by one: it rings every

## Natural frequencies: Gigerenzer's remedy

The problem turns transparent when counted in people instead of
percentages. Out of every 1000 people:

- 1 is sick. The test catches her with 99% probability: 1 true
  positive (almost always).
- 999 are healthy. The test fails on 5% of them: about 50 false
  positives.

Total positives: roughly 51. Sick among them: 1. The probability we
want, the <Term k="VPP">positive predictive value</Term>, is
1/51 ≈ 0.02.

Gigerenzer and Hoffrage (1995) showed this format,
<Term k="frecuencias naturales">natural frequencies</Term>, improves
answers substantially: correct responses rose from about 16% to 46%.
A big gain, but not a cure: most people still got it wrong. What the
experiment proves is that the format of the information weighs as
much as the ability to reason about it. This finding is the first
act of the debate module M7 takes seriously.

<Callout type="note" title="Minimal notation">
Three symbols and nothing more repeat through the whole course.
**P(A)** is the probability that A happens: a number between 0 and
1. **P(A | B)** is the probability of A *given that* B already
happened: the bar reads "given that" and shrinks the world to the
cases where B occurred. **P(A ∩ B)** is the probability of both
happening at once. That's enough to read every formula in the
course: P(sick | +) reads "probability of being sick given that the
test came back positive".
</Callout>

## Bayes' theorem as bookkeeping

The formula sorts exactly the same count:

$$
P(\text{sick} \mid +) = \frac{\text{sens} \cdot \text{prev}}
{\text{sens} \cdot \text{prev} + (1 - \text{spec}) \cdot (1 - \text{prev})}
$$

In words: up top go the sick people the test catches; down below,
all the positives, true plus false. The fraction is "of everyone who
tested positive, what share is actually sick".

The <Term k="prior">prior</Term> is the <Term k="prevalencia">prevalence</Term>:
what you believed before the test. The <Term k="verosimilitud">likelihood</Term>
is what the test says about each hypothesis. The <Term k="posterior">posterior</Term>
is the updated belief. Bayes doesn't guess: it keeps the books of
belief.

The code writes that bookkeeping as a function of prevalence and
draws it twice. On the left, the PPV curve over a logarithmic axis,
announced on the label, with the test pinned at its two ends:
prevalence of 1/1000 and of 50%. On the right, the concrete
question: out of every hundred positives, how many are truly sick
and how many are false alarms. The test doesn't change between
panels; what changes is the base rate.

Look at the graph, not just the numbers. On the left, the PPV curve:
the same test that is "95% accurate" is worth 2% at a prevalence of
one in a thousand and 95% at one in two. On the right, the concrete
question: of every 100 positives, how many are sick? At a prevalence
of 1/10 000 the ochre band nearly vanishes beneath the sand: 99.8%
of positives are false alarms. The test didn't change between
panels. The <Term k="tasa base">base rate</Term> did, and with it the
whole meaning of a positive result.

## The odds shortcut

The same bookkeeping has a pocket version, the most portable one for
a professional: count in <Term k="odds">odds</Term> instead of
probabilities. The prior odds of being sick are 1 to 999 (prevalence
1/1000). The test states how much more likely a positive is in a
sick person than in a healthy one: 0.99 / 0.05 = 19.8. The rule is a
multiplication:

$$
\text{posterior odds} = \text{prior odds} \times
\text{likelihood ratio}
$$

1 to 999 times 19.8 gives odds of 1 to 50: one sick person for every
fifty healthy among the positives, the same 2% as before without
touching the full formula. Kahneman recommends exactly this mental
move: anchor on the base rate and adjust only for the diagnostic
power of the evidence.

When the count gets inverted, the consequence stops being academic.
In 1999 Sally Clark was convicted of killing her two sons on expert
testimony that calculated "1 in 73 million": the probability of two
independent crib deaths in one family. The error was double. That
figure multiplied probabilities that were not independent, and above
all it confused P(deaths | innocence) with P(innocence | deaths):
the <Term k="falacia del fiscal">prosecutor's fallacy</Term>. Clark
was freed on appeal, destroyed, and died four years later. A
forgotten base rate can cost you points on an exam; an inverted
conditional cost her far more.

## When intuition misses the scale

The pattern is general and repeats wherever there is detection:
bank fraud (legitimate transactions swamp the fraudulent ones),
screening for rare diseases, security alarms, doping tests. Whenever
the sought-after class is rare, the false positives of the big group
outnumber the true positives of the small one. The test can be
excellent and the result still be, most of the time, a false alarm.

<Callout type="note" title="The same error in your field">
Screening is the same theorem in another uniform: a drug test with
99% accuracy at a company where 1 in 500 employees uses produces
more marked innocents than guilty; a credit score that approves the
segment where default is rare will inflate its false positives in
the rare segment. And the courtroom version costs freedom: the
probability of the evidence given innocence, not of innocence given
the evidence, is the number the expert should report and almost
never does.
</Callout>

## What comes next

You've now seen the same problem in two formats: probabilities that
confuse and natural frequencies that clarify. The icon array and the
PPV curve above are two faces of the same theorem. Come back to them
when you finish the module and compare your new estimate with the
one you recorded on entry: the distance between them is the measure
of what you learned. M3 moves from calculation to calibration: how
good your own confidence intervals are.

**In one sentence:** the probability of the evidence given the case is not the probability of the case given the evidence; the base rate is the denominator nobody skips.
